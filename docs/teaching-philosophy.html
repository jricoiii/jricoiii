Teaching Philosophy

I want students to leave my classes trusting their own thinking a little more. That starts with a classroom where everyone has time to notice something, ask a question, and try an idea. I’ve taught students with very different experiences of mathematics. Everyone deserves to be taken seriously while they’re figuring something out.

Start with something we can investigate

In Statistics, we might start with a set of wages and ask what seems fair to compare. We look at the data, talk about what we notice, and decide what a graph can help us say. In Math for Liberal Arts, we might turn four tiles around and ask how many patterns we can make. Organizing the patterns helps us see what we were missing. Then we can change a rule and see what happens.

I want students to have more chances to show their work and talk through what they saw. When a student points out something I overlooked, that is a good moment to slow down and let the class follow their thinking. I use what students show me to decide where we need more time, another example, or a different question.

A few things to try as a student

  • Start with one example you can draw, calculate, or explain. Say what you notice before trying to finish the whole problem.
  • Tell a classmate where you got stuck. Explaining what you tried is often enough to give you both a next move.
  • Ask a question early, in class, during office hours, or at the Learning Center. Give yourself time to revisit what you learned.
